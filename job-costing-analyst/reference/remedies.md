# Remedies

The Consultant's catalog. For each failure mode in `failure-modes.md`, two things:

- **The fix** — the system that, had it existed, would have stopped this. Stated as an action, narrow enough to actually do. Never a platitude.
- **Recovery** — whether *this* job's lost money can still be collected, or whether the lesson is all that survives.

Map the [ROOT] cause to its mode, take the remedy here, and **adapt it to the job's specifics** — the receipt, the name, the number. This catalog keeps the advice grounded; it is not a script to paste. One remedy for the root cause; a single line for each secondary finding.

The recovery verdict matters as much as the fix. Half of these are *not* recoverable after the fact, and saying "you can probably still bill for it" when you can't is malpractice. When the honest answer is "the lesson is the only thing you get back," say that.

---

## A — Estimating-stage

**A1 — Scope assumption error.**
*Fix:* a verify-before-you-price step for the one assumption that drives the bid — count the layers, pull the panel cover, probe the soil, open one wall. Put the unknown in the bid as an allowance or an exclusion, in writing, so a wrong guess becomes the customer's cost, not the contractor's.
*Recovery:* usually **not recoverable** — the price was given firm and the work is done. If the bid carried a written "assumes single layer" exclusion, the overage is billable; if it didn't, it's absorbed. The fix protects the *next* bid, not this one.

**A2 — Omitted line.**
*Fix:* a standard cost checklist for the trade, run against every bid before it goes out — the recurring lines that get forgotten live on it (crane, dumpster, permit, disposal, specialty rental). The omission isn't carelessness; it's the absence of a list.
*Recovery:* **not recoverable** on a firm-priced closed job. The omitted cost was real and is spent.

**A3 — Stale pricing.**
*Fix:* a shelf life on every quote — "valid 30 days" on the customer-facing bid, and a re-price check before ordering if the job books later than that. Tie the customer's price to a supplier quote *of the same age*; when the supplier quote expires, the customer quote does too.
*Recovery:* **rarely recoverable** — the firm price was honored, which is why it hurt. If the bid carried an escalation clause, the increase is billable; most small-shop bids don't.

**A4 — Overhead never loaded.**
*Fix:* rebuild the hourly/price basis to carry a share of overhead, computed from last year's actual overhead ÷ billable hours. This is a *pricing-model* fix, not a job fix — flag that it will change every future bid, not just this trade.
*Recovery:* **not recoverable**, and note the scope carefully: this job may have hit every direct-cost line. The loss is real but sits at the business level, so don't oversell a single-job fix. The remedy is a new price basis going forward.

## B — Execution-stage

**B1 — Rework / callbacks.**
*Fix:* find the specific point of failure and put a check there — a pre-inspection walkthrough against the code checklist before calling the inspector, a QC step before the crew leaves. Rework is a quality gate that wasn't there; name the gate, not "be more careful."
*Recovery:* **not recoverable** — rework is work the contractor eats by definition. Occasionally a defective material is a supplier warranty claim; check whether the redo was the crew's error or a bad part.

**B2 — Productivity miss.**
*Fix:* depends on the driver, and the day log usually names it — weather (build a weather buffer into the schedule/bid for exposed trades), a green helper (the estimate assumed a rate the crew couldn't hit — price to the real crew, not the ideal one). Don't prescribe "work faster."
*Recovery:* **not recoverable.** The hours are spent. The fix is a more honest hours estimate next time.

**B3 — Wrong burdened labor rate.**
*Fix:* rebuild the loaded rate from the real numbers — base wage + payroll tax + workers' comp + benefits + non-billable time — and bid off *that*, not the bare wage. `frameworks.md` has the build-up. This is the highest-leverage fix in the catalog because it silently corrects every future bid at once.
*Recovery:* **not recoverable** on this job, but flag the scope: if the rate was wrong here it is wrong on *every* open and future job, so the fix is worth far more than this one job's bleed. This is the one to press hardest.

## C — Billing-stage

**C1 — Unbilled change orders / scope creep.**
*Fix:* a change-order gate — no out-of-scope work starts until a one-line written CO with a price is approved. The medium can stay informal (a text is fine); what's missing is the *price* and the *line on the invoice*, not the formality. Wire the approved COs to the final invoice so nothing agreed-to fails to get billed.
*Recovery:* **sometimes recoverable, with a judgment call.** If the job just closed and the relationship is warm, a friendly "we forgot to add the approved extras" invoice can land — customers who said "do whatever it needs" often expect to pay. But weigh it against the relationship: a glowing review and a referral already banked (Hendricks) can be worth more than the recovery. Name the tradeoff; let the operator choose.

**C2 — Under-collection.**
*Fix:* a closeout step that reconciles final invoice against contract before it's sent — the discount-to-smooth-a-complaint and the rounded-down number both get caught by one "does this match the contract?" check.
*Recovery:* **possibly recoverable** if recent and the shortfall wasn't a deliberate goodwill discount. If it was a discount the operator chose, it's not an error to recover — it's a decision to price into the next bid.

**C3 — Retainage / final draw never collected.**
*Fix:* a receivables tickler — retainage and final draws go on a dated follow-up list the day they're held, not trusted to memory.
*Recovery:* **usually recoverable** — this is the most recoverable failure in the catalog. The money is owed, documented, and merely unchased. The remedy and the recovery are the same action: send the bill.

**C4 — Unbilled extras.**
*Fix:* a ticket-it-now habit backed by a standing rate sheet — trip charges, after-hours, small runs get logged at the moment they happen, because they're individually too small to remember and collectively the whole bleed. Name the *pattern*, not the individual trips.
*Recovery:* **rarely recoverable** individually — each is small and undocumented after the fact. The fix is systemic capture going forward.

---

## The through-line

Notice how many verdicts are "not recoverable." That is the honest shape of job costing: **most of these failures are only fixable forward.** The value of the diagnosis is not usually this job's money back — it's that the same leak, correctly named, stops draining every job after it. When the recovery is real (C3, often C1), say so plainly. When it isn't, don't manufacture false hope to end on an up note; the forward fix is the real deliverable, and it's enough.
